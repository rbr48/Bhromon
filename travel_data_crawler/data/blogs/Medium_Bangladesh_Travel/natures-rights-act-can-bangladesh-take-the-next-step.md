On October 23, in the UK House of Lords, Green Party Lord Natalie Bennett introduced the Nature’s Rights Bill. This landmark initiative seeks to recognize nature as a living entity with inherent rights, a concept environmentalists have championed for decades. The private member’s bill proposes a radical shift: changing the legal status of nature from objects, property, and resources to a legal subject with its own rights.
If enacted, the law would establish a
legal duty of care towards nature
and create a governance framework of national and bioregional councils to implement, monitor, and enforce its provisions. These measures aim to promote
sustainable and regenerative practices across all sectors of the economy and society.
The initiative is supported by prominent environmentalists including Chris Packham and Dale Vince, and will be submitted by former Green Party leader Baroness Natalie Bennett. For decades, lawyers, campaigners, and communities have worked tirelessly to bring the idea of
rights for nature and legal personhood
into public consciousness. The introduction of a fully drafted bill marks a historic moment, signaling that the Rights of Nature movement has moved from the margins to the center of democratic debate.

Bangladesh: Ready for the Next Step
This milestone in the UK raises an important question for Bangladesh: can we take a similar step? The answer is yes. Bangladesh already has constitutional and legal foundations that could allow for such a law.
Recognizing nature’s rights here would safeguard rivers, forests, wetlands, and hills, while reinforcing our commitment to sustainable development and environmental justice for generations to come.
“Recognizing nature’s rights in Bangladesh is not just about protecting forests or rivers — it is about securing our own future.”

Bangladesh’s Legal Milestones on Nature’s Rights
Bangladesh’s Constitution, under Article 18A, obliges the state to protect and improve the environment for both present and future generations. It also mandates the safeguarding of natural resources, biodiversity, wetlands, forests, and wildlife.
In 2019, the High Court recognized rivers as living entities, affirming that violating their rights is illegal. This set a strong legal precedent, signaling that the idea of Nature’s Rights is fully compatible with Bangladesh’s legal framework.
Later that year, in November 2019, the Bangladesh National Parliament made history by unanimously passing the
Planetary Emergency Motion
. This landmark resolution recognized the climate crisis as a planetary emergency, highlighting the urgent threats posed by climate change, biodiversity loss, and environmental degradation. The motion called for emergency-level action across government, civil society, and the private sector, integrating sustainability and resilience into national development planning.
It was a significant outcome of the
Youth Climate Justice Movement
, reflecting the persistent advocacy and mobilization of young climate activists demanding urgent governmental action. By linking parliamentary action with ecological responsibility, Bangladesh positioned itself as a global leader, showing that even climate-vulnerable nations can take bold steps to protect the planet. The motion also underscored the role of youth climate movements, symbolized by striking students outside the Parliament, emphasizing that urgent action is both a moral and political imperative.
Towards a Nature’s Rights Act
A Nature’s Rights Act in Bangladesh could formally recognize forests, rivers, wetlands, coasts, hills, and dunes as entities with the right to
exist, flourish, and regenerate
. Humans would act as
stewards
, responsible for protecting and sustaining these natural systems. Such a law would not only
strengthen environmental governance
but also align Bangladesh with the growing global movement that recognizes
nature’s legal personhood
.

Eco-Men: Redefining Environmental Leadership
Protecting nature is about people as much as laws.
The Eco-Men initiative engages men and boys in climate action and community-based conservation
, challenging traditional gender norms.
In regions like Shyamnagar, Satkhira, men participate alongside women in protecting rivers, forests, and wetlands. They contribute to sustainable practices, restoration projects, and climate adaptation efforts, demonstrating that environmental stewardship is a shared responsibility.
“Engaging men as allies, agents of change and beneficiaries of gender equality and in environmental protection strengthens community resilience and fosters a culture of shared responsibility.”

Rethinking Development: Nature and Wellbeing at the Core
Bangladesh is exploring new ideas to shift the course of development. Instead of measuring progress solely through GDP, the country envisions an economic model where
human well-being, sustainability of natural resources, and environmental balance are key indicators.
Experts warn that GDP-centric models degrade nature and increase inequality. Ahead of COP30, civil society has proposed a
“Nature and Wellbeing-based Economy,”
recognizing forests, rivers, and biodiversity as rights-bearing entities shaping development planning. The goal is to advance
both human life and environmental protection together.
A New Democratic Age: Youth-Led Activism and 2026 Elections
The youth-led mass uprisings of 2024 marked a turning point in Bangladesh’s democracy. Millions mobilized to demand accountability, transparency, and systemic change. Building on this momentum, the
2026 national parliament elections
offer a historic opportunity.
Political parties can integrate progressive environmental commitments into their manifestos, including formal recognition of nature’s rights, sustainable development frameworks, and climate justice policies. With public support and youth engagement, this new democratic age can become a platform to enact transformative environmental laws reflecting the values of both present and future generations.
“The energy of young citizens can drive Bangladesh towards a future where democracy and environmental justice walk hand in hand.”

Nature’s Rights Act: Why This Matters
Bangladesh is on the frontlines of climate change. Rising seas, riverbank erosion, extreme weather events, and biodiversity loss threaten livelihoods, food security, and human well-being. Recognizing nature’s rights is both an ecological and social imperative.
A Nature’s Rights Act, alongside initiatives like Eco-Men, would ensure that environmental protection is
holistic, inclusive, and sustainable
. Internationally, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) and other legal bodies increasingly emphasize that states have binding responsibilities to protect the environment and safeguard the rights of current and future generations. This principle is rooted in international law, including the Stockholm Declaration (1972), the Rio Declaration on Environment and Development (1992), and UN conventions on climate change and biodiversity. Under these frameworks, environmental protection is not merely a policy choice but a legal obligation, encompassing duties to prevent transboundary harm, conserve ecosystems, and adopt sustainable development practices.
Following the ICJ’s advisory opinion, Bangladesh faces a clear imperative to enact strong and comprehensive climate legislation that ensures equity in implementing its Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), protects vulnerable communities, and guarantees justice for future generations. On 23 July, the ICJ delivered this landmark advisory opinion from The Hague, addressing critical issues such as climate finance, compensation, and adaptation. The court warned that failure to provide adequate compensation for climate damages could expose responsible states to legal accountability, underscoring that climate action is not only a moral obligation but also a binding legal responsibility under international law.
Climate justice, therefore, is both a legal and ethical mandate. It recognizes that the impacts of climate change disproportionately affect vulnerable communities and future generations, creating a moral and legal duty for states and corporations to act urgently. By embedding these principles into national legislation, such as a proposed Nature’s Rights Act, Bangladesh can align its domestic legal framework with international obligations, ensuring that environmental protection is enforced through law rather than left to voluntary action.

Call to Action
I call on the interim government, political parties, civil society, and decision-makers to rise to this moment. Use existing constitutional and legal tools to recognize nature as a living entity with rights. Support initiatives like Eco-Men that bring communities into environmental stewardship. Protecting rivers, forests, wetlands, coasts, hills, and dunes is inseparable from protecting human life.
Now is the time to act.
Bangladesh can lead the world again by showing that a climate-vulnerable nation can champion
environmental justice, Nature’s Rights, and inclusive stewardship.
Together, we can secure a future where humans and nature.
